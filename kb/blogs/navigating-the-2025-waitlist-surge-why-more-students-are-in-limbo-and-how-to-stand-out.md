# Introduction

If it feels like more students than ever are being waitlisted this year… you're not imagining things. The 2025 college admissions cycle has brought with it a wave of uncertainty, stress, and what we can only describe as waitlist madness . Students with impeccable records, test scores, and accomplishments are being told "not yet" - not denied, but not accepted either. It's a confusing and frustrating limbo.

# Why Are So Many Students on the Waitlist This Year?

The explosion of waitlist offers in 2025 is no accident. Several key factors are shaping this year's admissions landscape:

- Application surges : Thanks to the Common App and test-optional policies, schools are seeing record numbers of applicants. More applications mean lower admit rates, and more students are pushed to the waitlist as colleges hedge their bets. As many admitted students still weigh their options across multiple offers, schools waitlist strong candidates as a backup while they wait to find out how many admitted students will actually commit. Source: TheaStudy

- Yield protection : Colleges are increasingly focused on their yield rate -the percentage of admitted students who actually choose to enroll compared to the total number of students they admitted. A low yield can make a school appear less desirable, which can affect rankings and prestige. To protect this number, some colleges waitlist even strong applicants if they suspect those students may choose another school, preferring to offer admission only to those likely to say yes. Source: Collegiate Gateway

- Institutional priorities : Colleges are juggling their own goals - geographic diversity, full-pay students, legacy admissions, athletics, and more. In some cases, even highly qualified students might be waitlisted simply because of fit. Adding to the complexity, uncertainties around research funding under the new presidential administration may impact faculty hiring in certain disciplines. Fewer professors could mean fewer available spots in specific majors, prompting schools to be more cautious about the number of students they admit in those fields. Source: Inside Higher Ed

- Unpredictable student behavior : Post-pandemic shifts in student decision-making, combined with economic factors like unexpected financial aid outcomes, have made enrollment harder to predict. If students don't receive the aid they need or expect, they may opt for more affordable options. As a result, colleges are keeping more students "on deck" to maintain flexibility and ensure they can fill their incoming class. Source: Market Watch

# If You've Been Waitlisted… What Now?

First, take a deep breath. Being waitlisted is not a rejection - it means you are a strong candidate. But it is important to act strategically. Here's what you can do:

Decide if you want to stay on the waitlist:

Some students already feel committed elsewhere, while others want to keep the door open. If you're considering staying on a waitlist, do a little research. You can check your chances of being admitted by reviewing a college's Common Data Set-a standardized report most colleges publish. Search "[College Name] + Common Data Set," then navigate to Section C: "First-Time, First-Year (Freshman) Admission" and look for the waitlist data (item C2). There you'll see how many students were offered a spot, how many accepted, and how many were ultimately admitted. If the college notes that its waitlist is ranked, that means some waitlisted students are prioritized over others-so a strong, specific LOCI may help you stand out. Even if the waitlist isn't ranked, this data can give you a rough sense of your odds and help guide your decision.

Send a strong Letter of Continued Interest (LOCI):

This isn't just another update letter. Unlike a general mid-year update you may have sent in January or February, your LOCI should give the college a clear reason to admit you now. In addition to reaffirming your interest and sharing meaningful new accomplishments, highlight the specific contributions you would make to that specific college community: Is there a professor whose work aligns with your experience? A campus organization, service initiative, or lab where you can immediately contribute? The more concrete and personalized your letter, the easier it is for admissions officers to see how you would strengthen and add value to their campus community.

Don't delay:

Submit your LOCI as soon as possible. Waiting until the deadline can come across as lukewarm interest. A prompt response shows genuine enthusiasm and commitment.

Reach out with discretion:

If you have a meaningful connection-like a regional rep or alumni interviewer-you can let them know you're still interested. Just be mindful not to overwhelm the admissions office with repeated contact.

Submit an enrollment deposit elsewhere:

Secure your spot at another school you're excited about. If you're later admitted off the waitlist to a school you prefer, you can reassess your options.
